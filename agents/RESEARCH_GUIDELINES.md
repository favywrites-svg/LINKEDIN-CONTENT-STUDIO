# Research Guidelines

## Purpose

Research exists to help the author form a useful, informed perspective. It is not a feed of headlines to repost.

## Research areas

- AI implementation and business adoption.
- Business operations, process optimization, and automation opportunities.
- AI agents and agentic workflows.
- Healthcare operations, digital health, and pharmaceutical technology.
- Relevant technology developments and their practical implications.
- Problems and lessons emerging from the author's confirmed projects and experiments.

## Source priority

Use this order of preference where relevant:

1. Primary sources: official product documentation, standards, regulatory guidance, original announcements, filings, datasets, and first-party technical materials.
2. Original research: peer-reviewed papers, research institutions, universities, and recognized research organizations.
3. Credible reporting and analysis: established technology, business, and industry publications.
4. Expert commentary and professional discussions: useful for understanding practitioner concerns, but not sufficient alone for high-stakes factual claims.

Examples of sources that may be useful include official WHO materials, regulatory agencies, Nature, MIT Technology Review, and established technology/business publications. This is not an exclusive or mandatory list. Select sources based on the specific claim.

## Research process

1. Define the question or problem before searching.
2. Search for the primary source first.
3. Find independent corroboration for important or surprising claims.
4. Check publication date and whether the information is still current.
5. Read the relevant source material, not just the search snippet.
6. Record the exact source URL and dates.
7. Extract only evidence relevant to the proposed content angle.
8. Note uncertainty, limitations, conflicts, and missing context.
9. Translate the finding into business or implementation implications.
10. Propose a content angle that adds interpretation.

## Research record fields

For each source/finding, capture:
- Title.
- Publisher/organization.
- URL.
- Publication date, if available.
- Date accessed.
- Source type (primary, research, reporting, commentary).
- Main findings.
- Exact facts or statistics that may be used.
- Relevant limitations or caveats.
- Why it matters to businesses or professionals.
- Potential content angles.
- Verification status.

## Fact-checking rules

- Never invent a source, URL, publication date, quotation, statistic, or study result.
- Do not treat search snippets as verified evidence.
- Check the original study or document before using a statistic whenever possible.
- Preserve context, sample size, geography, time period, and methodology when relevant.
- Avoid causal claims when a source only shows correlation.
- Treat vendor claims as vendor claims unless independently validated.
- Flag conflicting evidence rather than selecting whichever source supports the preferred angle.
- Distinguish what a source says from what the author may reasonably infer.
- Do not present outdated information as current.

## From research to authority

For every promising finding, answer:
- What is the actual business or human problem?
- Why should the intended reader care?
- What might a business overlook?
- What does implementation involve beyond buying a tool?
- What risks, costs, workflow changes, or human checks matter?
- What is the author's distinct contribution?

If the only possible post is "Company X launched Y", it is not yet a strong content opportunity.

## Research depth

Use moderate research for ordinary content opportunities: inspect relevant sources, identify key evidence, and find useful examples.

Use deeper research for:
- Important industry developments.
- Technical case studies.
- Healthcare or pharmaceutical claims.
- High-impact statistics.
- Controversial or disputed claims.

Do not over-research simple opinion or reflection posts. Ask the author for their perspective instead.

## Research access and free-tier constraints

The application should use available free research/search options where possible. Do not assume a particular free API is unlimited or permanently free.

If a search provider is unavailable, rate-limited, or requires payment:
- Explain the limitation.
- Preserve work already entered.
- Offer manual URL/source entry as a fallback.
- Do not silently switch to a paid provider.
- Do not fabricate research results.

## Research output format

A research synthesis should include:
1. Question investigated.
2. Key findings.
3. Evidence and source links.
4. What the findings mean.
5. Business or human problem exposed.
6. Implementation considerations and limitations.
7. Potential LinkedIn angles.
8. Questions for the author, if personal input is needed.
