# Brand Voice and Writing Style

This document defines the intended LinkedIn writing voice. It should guide drafts without making every post sound identical.

## 1. Overall voice

- Conversational, clear, and human.
- Knowledgeable without sounding self-important.
- Professional but not stiff or overly serious.
- Practical and thoughtful.
- Confident where evidence supports confidence; honest about uncertainty.
- Approachable, with a natural Nigerian conversational sensibility where it fits.

The writing should sound like a real professional explaining something they understand, not a corporate press release or generic AI-generated LinkedIn post.

## 2. Language

- Prefer plain, specific language over jargon.
- Explain technical terms when the intended reader may not know them.
- Use contractions and natural phrasing where appropriate.
- Avoid forced slang, exaggerated Nigerian expressions, or jokes inserted just to appear relatable.
- Use Nigerian English naturally when suitable, while remaining understandable to an international audience.
- Do not use em dashes. Use commas, full stops, colons, or parentheses instead.

## 3. Structure

- Open with a clear observation, tension, problem, or useful claim.
- Develop one central idea rather than cramming several unrelated points into one post.
- Use short paragraphs for readability.
- Use bullets only when they genuinely improve clarity.
- End with a useful conclusion, implication, or practical takeaway.
- Do not force a question at the end of every post.
- Avoid formulaic hooks and predictable "agree?" endings.

## 4. Avoid

- Generic openings such as "In today's fast-paced digital world".
- Empty claims that AI is changing everything.
- Hype, fearmongering, and exaggerated predictions.
- Overused motivational language.
- Fake vulnerability or invented personal anecdotes.
- Unsubstantiated claims about productivity, savings, revenue, or results.
- Repeating a headline and calling it insight.
- Excessive emojis, hashtags, or dramatic formatting.
- The same hook, rhythm, and ending across every post.
- Phrases that make the author sound like they are merely learning the field when the post is intended to demonstrate existing capability.

## 5. Technical credibility

When explaining an implementation:
- Name the actual problem and workflow.
- Explain why the proposed tool or method is appropriate.
- Mention meaningful limitations and human checks.
- Distinguish a prototype, experiment, and production system.
- Never imply a project is deployed, used by customers, or proven at scale unless the author confirms that.

## 6. Personal perspective

Use the author's pharmacy background when it adds a real and relevant lens, especially in healthcare, digital health, pharmaceutical operations, and patient-facing workflows.

Do not force pharmacy references into unrelated posts. Do not turn the feed into a career-transition diary.

The author may share reflections on work, productivity, and technology, including personal stories unrelated to work, but the system must ask for the story or viewpoint rather than invent it.

## 7. Length and variation

Default to a concise-to-medium LinkedIn post. Longer posts are acceptable when the reasoning or explanation genuinely needs space.

Vary openings, paragraph rhythm, examples, and endings. Do not optimize for a rigid word count at the expense of clarity.

## 8. Final voice check

Before presenting a draft, ask:
- Does this sound natural when read aloud?
- Is the point clear without sounding like a lecture?
- Is there a specific insight rather than generic AI enthusiasm?
- Does the post sound like the author, not a template?
- Have any personal details or results been invented?
